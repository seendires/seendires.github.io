SEENDiReS

Spectrum and Energy Efficient Networks for Disaster Resilient Societies

After the February 2023 earthquakes in Türkiye, mobile networks were unreliable for the first three to four days, the most critical period for search and rescue. SEENDiReS develops models, predictions and network protocols that keep people connected when power is lost, sites go down and demand surges.

Who benefits

People who cannot wait for the network to come back

In the first days after a disaster, a working connection decides how fast help arrives.

Search and rescue teams

Need reliable links to coordinate in the first 72 hours.

SEENDiReS predicts where demand will rise and keeps capacity there.

Survivors and families

Need to call for help and reach relatives when everyone calls at once.

SEENDiReS uses spectrum more efficiently so fewer calls are blocked.

Aid organisations and authorities

Need timely information to deliver aid and send warnings.

SEENDiReS forecasts damage to sites and power so the network can plan ahead.

Network operators

Need to keep sites on air while running on batteries and generators.

SEENDiReS stretches battery life with sleep and power control.

The problem

Less power, fewer sites, far more demand, all at once

Energy

Earthquakes cut grid power and fuel supply. Sites run on batteries and generators, so every watt decides how long the network stays up.

Spectrum

Rescue teams, survivors and aid organisations all try to call at the same moment. Spectrum plans built for normal days congest within minutes.

Infrastructure

Rooftop sites fall with their buildings, debris and flooding change radio propagation, and emergency units may work only on certain bands.

Research

Keeping networks up when the city is not

Disasters cut power, damage sites and multiply demand at the same time. SEENDiReS develops the models, predictions and protocols needed to keep service going through those changes.

ChallengeWork packagesTimelineTestbeds and toolsGlossary

The challenge

Networks built for normal days

After a large earthquake, grid power and fuel are scarce, so energy decides how long sites stay on air. At the same time, rescue teams, survivors and aid organisations all try to communicate, and the surge congests spectrum plans designed for normal traffic.

Base station density that maximises energy efficiency is lower than the density that maximises spectral efficiency. Existing cellular networks are rigid, cell-centric and always on, so they cannot raise both at once. They also react to failures after they happen.

Most work on post-disaster networks relies on aerial or satellite platforms. Less attention has gone to making the surviving terrestrial infrastructure work efficiently, and to the effect of cascading power outages and backhaul damage.

Work packages

Three work packages, each feeding the next

WP1 provides the efficiency models used by the predictions of WP2. Both drive the self-organising protocols of WP3.

WP1M1–M12 · TAMU leads, GTU

Spectrum and energy efficiency models with disaster consideration

T1.1 · M1–M6
Analytical models for SE and EE. TAMU builds the general model of spectral and energy efficiency for different node distributions and power consumption models. GTU identifies how a disaster changes these parameters.
T1.2 · M7–M12
Joint optimisation with user device energy. The model adds the energy used by user devices, then GTU adds the cost of energy in the post-disaster scenario.

D1.1 · M6 · Analytical model for network SE and EE
D1.2 · M12 · Joint optimisation of SE and EE with UE energy constraints

WP2M13–M24 · GTU leads, TAMU

Predictive techniques for disaster mitigation on communication infrastructure

T2.1 · M13–M18
Post-disaster communication demand. A predictive model built on historical data by GTU, evaluated with statistical metrics developed by TAMU.
T2.2 · M16–M21
Infrastructure damage. A probabilistic model of road closures, power outages and base station failures, validated on past disasters or simulated scenarios.
T2.3 · M19–M24
Post-disaster propagation. A channel model that accounts for debris and flooding, validated through simulation and available measurements.

D2.1 · M18 · Predictive model for post-disaster communication demand
D2.2 · M24 · Predictive model for infrastructure damage and propagation channel

WP3M25–M36 · GTU and TAMU

Physical and MAC layer designs for efficient communication systems

T3.1 · M25–M30
Self-organising strategies for extended service. Protocols with distributed power control and sleep and wake mechanisms, designed by TAMU and compared with existing protocols by GTU.
T3.2 · M31–M36
PHY and MAC techniques. Ambient backscatter and LoRa compared on data rate, coverage range and energy efficiency in different environments.

D3.1 · M30 · Self-organising network strategies for extended service
D3.2 · M36 · Comparison of PHY/MAC techniques in SE and EE

Glossary

Terms used on this site

SE · Spectral efficiency
Data delivered per unit of spectrum, in bit/s/Hz. Area spectral efficiency (ASE) adds per unit of area.
EE · Energy efficiency
Data delivered per unit of energy, in bit/J. Here it covers both the network and user devices.
BS · Base station
The radio site that serves mobile users. A site may sit on a rooftop or on its own mast.
UE · User equipment
A phone or other device connected to the network.
SON · Self-organising network
A network that changes its own settings, such as transmit power or sleep, without manual intervention.
RRH · Remote radio head
The radio unit of a base station. It can be put to sleep and woken to save energy.
MDRU · Movable and deployable resource unit
A portable network unit brought to a disaster area to restore service.
LoRa · Long range
A low-power, long-range radio technology for small amounts of data, part of the LPWAN family.
Ambient backscatter
Communication by reflecting existing radio signals instead of generating new ones, using almost no energy.
PHY and MAC
The physical layer (waveforms and modulation) and medium access control layer (who transmits when).
QoS and QoE
Quality of service as measured by the network, and quality of experience as felt by users.
Erlang
A unit of voice traffic. One Erlang is one channel busy for the whole period.

Datasets and tools

Open data and software

Real post-disaster network data is scarce. SEENDiReS will generate synthetic data from realistic scenarios and release its models and simulation tools.

WP2In progress

Synthetic post-disaster traffic dataset

Simulated mobile traffic and network indicators before, during and after an earthquake, at site and cell level.

  • Normal-period traffic with calendar effects such as holidays
  • Post-earthquake call and data surge, user movement to assembly areas
  • Site outages from building damage and power loss
WP2In progress

Post-disaster network simulator

City-scale simulation of a mobile network after a disaster, built on real building data and ray tracing.

  • Building damage and debris in the radio scene
  • Battery and generator behaviour of sites
  • Voice and data load per cell and technology
WP1Planned

SE and EE models and optimisation code

Analytical models and the joint SE/EE optimisation framework, including user device energy.

WP3Planned

Self-organising protocol simulations

Simulation code for power control and sleep and wake mechanisms under post-disaster traffic.

Release of open-source software and tools is planned for the second project year. Data about real users will be anonymised and minimised.
